6 - Speed and Numbers

How long things take and how fast they go, measured. Every number on this page comes from one run of a larger benchmark (Release build) on the machine below, so expect them to move a little from run to run: where it matters the page gives the middle of many runs and the slowest 1 in 20. That benchmark is kept outside the repo; dotnet run -c Release --project YuukoSandbox -- bench in the repo measures the first few rows yourself (the handshake, a question to a connected device, many at once, and raw AES-256-GCM).

  • Machine: AMD64 Family 23 Model 113 Stepping 0, AuthenticAMD, 8 logical cores, 15.9 GB memory
  • Microsoft Windows 10.0.19045, .NET 8.0.31
  • Date: 2026-11-12

What the numbers say (TLDR)

The crypto is cheap, except signing. A Kyber-1024 key operation is about a millisecond and verifying a Dilithium5 signature is about half a millisecond. Signing is slower and uneven: the middle is 2.5 ms for a small message but 1 in 20 takes 14 ms. That is why a handshake is 5 ms, and why signing every audit entry costs more than keying it. Making a whole identity (both key pairs) is about 5 ms and happens once.

Connecting is a one-time cost. The 5 ms handshake is Kyber, Dilithium5 and a throw-away key for forward secrecy. After it, asking a connected device something takes 40 µs because the encryption on an open session is fast: AES-256-GCM does about 300 MB/s on large messages (small messages are slower per byte, because each one has a fixed cost).

Big commands are limited by encrypting and copying them, not by the protocol. A 1 MB command takes 21 ms in-process (96 MB/s counting both directions). Over real Zenoh it is 47 ms: the extra is the socket.

Shared documents are quick for edits and fine for big ones. An edit reaches the other device in under 3 ms in the middle. A 10 MB document takes 0.3 s in pieces of 500 KB (33 MB/s). Three hundred small documents go out at about 400 a second, and a device that was away compares notes in pages of about 200 KB and gets 14 MB of documents in under a second.

Folders cost most per file, not per byte. A thousand small files is 225 files a second, and 4,100 is 140 a second: each file is its own request, its own hash and its own write. One big file is faster per byte (a 10 MB file takes 0.14 s and a 100 MB file 1.1 s, about 80–100 MB/s) but each 256 KB piece is asked for and answered one after the other, so it is limited by the round trip, not the disk. That means over a real network you add about one round trip per piece: 40 pieces for 10 MB and 400 for 100 MB (an estimate from the arithmetic, not a measurement: with a 20 ms round trip that is roughly +0.8 s and +8 s). Hashing a file alone runs at 1,400 MB/s from the cache, and looking at a folder of 4,100 files for the first time takes 3.4 s.

The Cogitarium sends each piece to 10 devices and they pass it on. A 10 KB piece reaches all 30 devices in 16 ms; a 100 KB piece in 78 ms; a 512 KB piece in 410 ms. A steady stream of 100 KB pieces goes at about 20 a second (2 MB/s to every device) with none missed, because the source sends each piece to its 10 first devices before the next one.

The audit log is as fast as the disk. Each entry is flushed to disk before Append returns, which is the 4 ms. Checking a keyed log runs at about 100,000 entries a second; checking a signed log is 1,750 a second because every signature is verified.
